📐 Is the RPR column usable today? Eight races, eight answers

The Cambridgeshire settled that the racecard’s RPR is a projection to each race’s par, not an achieved rating — so it has to be calibrated per race before it is allowed to carry an argument. Two diagnostics: ratio = RPR spread ÷ OR spread (low means compressed toward a par), and r(gap, OR) = how strongly the RPR−OR gap just tracks the mark downwards. A gap correlation near −1 means “well handicapped on the figures” is arithmetic that selects the outsider every time.

💰 Two of his prices differ from ours — and no, that is not a STEAM

The column quotes two prices that appear on our card, and they move in opposite directions: Turty Tree 6/1 against our 13/2 (shorter) and Musical Times 1/2 against our 4/9 (longer).

§6 has a rule that looks like it applies here, and its conditions are not met. “A price shorter than your card cannot be a best-odds artefact” holds because a price-comparison column only ever quotes the best of several books, which is longer by construction. William Hill is one bookmaker quoting its own board, and a single book can sit either side of a forecast on any horse. The asymmetry the rule rests on does not exist, so the inference is not available.

And §5b wants the book normalised before anything is called. Two prices are not a book: we cannot compute a margin, so we cannot tell a genuine contraction from one firm pricing a race tighter than the forecast. Recorded as two observations, called as neither. The Friday card still has no market trail, and saying so is the honest answer.

📐 The residual is not an instrument when the line is flat — and in the Cesarewitch it is flat

Every card since the Cambridgeshire has used the same fix: raw RPR is a projection to the race’s par, the RPR−OR gap is arithmetic that selects the outsider, and the usable figure is the residual against the race’s own OR→RPR line. Saturday breaks that, and it is worth writing down carefully.

The Cesarewitch fits a slope of +0.0016. That is not a weak line, it is a flat one — flatter than the −0.006 in the Cambridgeshire that produced the whole method. And a flat line has a consequence nobody had noticed: the residual degenerates into the raw RPR. With no slope, resid = RPR − mean(RPR), a constant shift. Measured on this race the correlation between residual and raw RPR is 0.999996 and the largest difference is 0.02lb.

So ranking by residual here is ranking by RPR — the exact thing the rule was written to stop. The instrument does not warn you: it returns a tidy ordered list with plausible numbers beside it, and that list is the projection you were trying to escape. The residual is only a reading when the line it is measured against has a slope. Check r(RPR, OR) first; below about 0.3 there is no line, so there is no residual either.

Consequence for the card: not one pick in the Cesarewitch or the nursery leans on a rating. They lead on course-and-distance form, the 4lb penalty that says a horse won since the weights, the handicapper’s own forward view (Future OR), Topspeed and the trainer. In the 16:15 there are no marks at all, so no calibration was run — §6 says a maiden leaves RPR untestable and that is stated on the race rather than quietly skipped.

Four of the seven races fit on five rated runners or fewer, and the Dewhurst on three. Those rows are printed because leaving them out would be the more flattering choice, but a three-point line is not evidence and nothing on this card rests on one. 🎯 The Cesarewitch shows 42 entries. That is not a LONG, and this is the race the archive warns about by name

The LONG slot has been shut in every race of the last five meetings because it needs a handicap of sixteen or more runners and none has appeared. The Cesarewitch is the one race left in 2026 that could open it — a 2m2f marathon handicap — and the entry list says 42.

So the temptation is at its maximum, and the answer is still no. Forty-two is an entry count. §9 records what happened the last time this engine reasoned from one: on 2026 entries, six of the St Leger Thursday’s eight races had 16 or more, and the Day 1 panel wrote “if even half of these hold their numbers, the LONG opens on Thursday.” Declarations cut it from six races to one. The card shed 158 entries to 85 declared and the slot was shut in all eight. Field size is the one thing an entry list tells you nothing about, and the LONG gate is a field-size test.

The evidence that it will shed is sitting on this same page. Friday’s card at this very meeting went 170 entries to 100 declared — 41% — two days ago. Apply that rate and 42 becomes about 25. That number is deliberately not printed as a forecast anywhere on this page, because producing it is the exact move §9 forbids. It is here once, in the paragraph explaining why it is worthless.

What the gate did, mechanically, is the part worth keeping. Run on this fixture it returned NO BET — 42 runner(s) without a draw and, beside it, “LONG slot: not assessed — race failed the gate, no confirmed field to test”. Until 9 September it would have printed “LONG slot: open” there, computing the place test off 42 entry-stage runners — arithmetic on a field that does not exist. That fix was made on a card that cost nothing; this is the race it was made for.

The question gets answered on 8 October, when declarations land, and not one hour before.

SCORED, 8 October. The Cesarewitch declared 29 and the LONG slot is open. The refusal was right on principle and the discipline cost nothing: the slot opens on the declared field, exactly as it would have anyway, and nothing was staked on a number that did not exist.

But score the estimate too, because it was wrong. The paragraph above noted that Friday’s 41% shed rate would put 42 entries at “about 25”. It declared 29 — a 31% shed, not 41% — so the figure we declined to publish was four runners light. It would not have changed the decision, since 25 clears sixteen as readily as 29 does, but that is luck rather than method: a four-runner error in the other direction at a lower entry count is exactly what closes a slot that should be open. A shed rate is not transferable between cards, even consecutive days at the same meeting.

🎯 The LONG slot — forecast in advance, and confirmed by declarations

The slot needs 5+ places paid (or 4+ in a field of 16 or more), a price of 8/1 or bigger, and a concrete signal. On the corrected place bands that reduces to one thing: a handicap of sixteen or more runners. Nothing else clears it.

At this meeting the candidate is the Cesarewitch. A 2m2f marathon handicap that routinely declares thirty or more — on the archive’s own arithmetic it is the likeliest race left in 2026 to open the LONG slot.

🏆 Newmarket — the Rowley Mile

Right-handed, wide and galloping, with one of the stiffest finishes in Britain. The straight course runs a full mile: it dips about a furlong and a half out and then rises to the line, which finds out anything that committed too early. Draw is close to immaterial in a small field but becomes a first-order factor in the big autumn handicaps, where a wide field splits into groups — the Cesarewitch is the standing example. Rule 17 applies in full: read the GoingStick strips, require about half a point of spread, re-read at the off. Going is free-draining chalk heath, so October ground is usually good or softer only after real rain.
