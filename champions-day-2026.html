★ ratio 0.62 and a −0.814 gap correlation — the level is compressed even though a line exists, so a big residual off a low mark is read with care. † fitted on six rated runners; §6 says print the row and let nothing rest on it.

⚠ The Balmoral found a new way for the residual to fail, and the gate let it through. §6 says check r(RPR,OR) before computing a residual, and that below about 0.3 there is no line. The Balmoral reads −0.326, whose absolute value clears the bar. It should not. A negative correlation means better-marked horses are projected lower — the par signature at its most extreme, not evidence of a line.
And the old degeneracy test would have missed it too. §6 flags a residual as degenerate when the slope b is near zero; here b = −0.150, nowhere near. Yet corr(residual, raw RPR) = +0.945 — ranking by residual is ranking by raw RPR, which is the one thing the method exists to prevent. The reason is reach, not slope: the line can move a runner by at most |b| × OR spread = 2.3lb against an RPR spread of 8lb, so it shifts the order by about a quarter and leaves it otherwise intact. Both tests are now fixed in the script — the first gate is signed, and degeneracy is measured as the line’s reach against the RPR spread.
What that costs, concretely. The raw gap and the raw RPR both hand you Excellent Believe and Fifth Column at the top of the Balmoral. So does the residual — because it is the same instrument. Both are rated 99, the joint-lowest mark in the race, shared with three others, and §6 records that the gap is biggest for the lowest mark by construction. Our two picks there lead on course-and-distance form and the handicapper’s Future OR instead.

1 · Apply the shorter-price test only where the column STATES its odds are a comparison. Where it does not say, you do not know what you are differencing. Record both observations and call neither.
2 · Normalise within a source. Never pool two. Pooling these two lists into one median produces a spurious STEAM/DRIFT split down the middle, because the “signal” is just which list a horse happened to come from.

🏆 Ascot — right-handed, stiff, galloping

A right-handed triangle of about 1m6f with a steady uphill pull from the home turn to the line, plus a straight mile that joins the round course. Nothing here is sharp and nothing is cheap: the finish is stiff enough that a horse in front two out can still be swallowed. On the Flat the draw matters in big straight-course handicaps, where the field splits and one side can be on materially better ground — rule 17 governs, so read the strips rather than a rule of thumb. Over jumps it is a galloping, stamina-favouring track that rewards a sound jumper who stays; the Long Walk is run over more than three miles and is won on stamina, not speed.
