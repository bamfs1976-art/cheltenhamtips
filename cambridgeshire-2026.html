📈 The market signal has been measured wrong all season, and this card shows why

Step 5b differences the Racing Post betting forecast against the board price on the same card. Do that raw on Friday and it reads 7 STEAM against 31 DRIFT — a card where almost everything is apparently being deserted. That is not money. It is arithmetic:

The board is looser than the forecast in all seven races, by about eight points of margin. A runner can therefore hold exactly the same share of the book and still read as a DRIFT, because the whole book got longer. We have been measuring the bookmaker’s margin and calling it money.

The fix is one line of arithmetic: express each price as its share of its own book normalised to 100%, then take the deviation from the race median. Do that and Friday reads 21 STEAM, 24 DRIFT, 21 flat — balanced, which is what a card with no news on it should look like. Every badge on this page is computed that way, and the genuine movers come out clearly:

Sugar Yes Please +33% in the Rockfel is the biggest single move on the card and reads as a mild shortening of 5/1→9/2 on the raw method. Coedana, Inis Mor, Without Prejudice and Venetia all held their prices in the Princess Royal while eight rivals drifted, which the raw method scores as flat and the corrected method scores as support. Against them, Runswick −19% and Quorum −16% are the two Spotlight selections the market is walking away from.

This is the third consecutive meeting where the market method has been found defective, and the first with a fix that can be applied immediately. The St Leger found that we measure forecast→racecard and never racecard→off; this finds that the leg we do measure is contaminated by margin. Both stay true and both need the same thing: two observations of the same market, normalised. The caveat, stated: this assumes the forecast and the board are honest statements of the same market at different times differing only in margin. A uniform eight-point gap across seven races is strong evidence for that, not proof.

🚩 Talk Of New York won, and the boost we priced this morning would have paid

This card opposed the 15:00 favourite and printed the reason before the off: “he is the best horse in his race on every column and 4/6 is probably about right; that is why he is not a bet… with only two places the each-way half is dead.” He won by 4¾ lengths at 4/9. Nick Luck wrote almost the same sentence and also left him out.

So was the reasoning wrong? Not on its own terms — we never claimed he would be beaten, and he was correctly identified as the best horse in the race. What the result exposes is narrower and more useful: a two-place race removes the only mechanism by which our staking plan can profit from being right about a short-priced horse. 50p each-way on a 4/6 chance in a two-place contest is a bet the plan cannot win well even when the analysis is perfect. The honest options were a win single or nothing, and the card chose a third thing — two other horses — which is how £2.00 became £0.00.

And the offer review this morning called it. bet365 boosted him from 4/7 to 1/1; this session assessed that at roughly +8p to +14p per £1 and said it was the best value on the screen, while flagging that £10 sat outside the staking plan. It would have returned double. That is recorded because the archive is only worth anything if the calls that landed outside the card get scored too.

Persica’s third is the other half of the same race. The card flagged two places and rule 6 in advance; being right about the terms did not stop a placed horse returning nothing. At three places he would have returned £1.10, which is most of the day’s deficit.

📈 Two standing findings tested, and only one of them actually gained anything

2 · The card→off market test gained almost nothing today, and the reason is worth stating rather than hiding. Four of our picks shortened between our price and the off, and all four made the frame. That looks like a third straight confirmation — but it is close to circular: starting prices are published for the first three and for named beaten favourites, so for most of these horses we only know they shortened because they placed. A steamer that ran badly is invisible to us.

The unbiased subset is the two named beaten favourites, and they split: Arisaig steamed from 4/1 into 3/1 favourite and was unplaced, Waterford Castle drifted 7/2 to 4/1 and was unplaced. One steamer lost, one drifter lost. Today adds one genuine data point, not four.

A pre-registered prediction that lands is worth more than a pattern found afterwards in a biased sample, and that is the whole difference between the two halves of this panel.

🏆 Newmarket — the Rowley Mile

Right-handed, wide and galloping, and one of the stiffest finishes in Britain. The straight course runs a mile with a dip about a furlong and a half out, then rises to the line — a test that finds out anything that committed too early. There are no tight bends and no camber tricks: like York and Doncaster it rewards the best horse rather than the best-positioned one, which is why short-priced class acts hold up here far better than at Chester or Epsom.

Draw. Close to immaterial in a small field. It becomes a first-order factor in the big autumn handicaps, where a wide field splits into two or more groups and one side can be racing on materially different ground — the Cambridgeshire is the standing example. The favoured side moves with the ground, the rail and the watering, so rule 17 applies in full: read the GoingStick strips, require about half a point of spread before letting it move a pick, and re-read at the off.

Going. Newmarket sits on free-draining chalk heath and dries quickly, so late-September ground is usually good or quicker unless it has rained properly. Check it on the morning of each card; do not carry it forward from the previous day.
